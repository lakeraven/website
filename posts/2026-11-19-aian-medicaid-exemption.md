*Last reviewed 20 November 2026. We update this post as federal rules and state policies change. Sources are numbered and listed under [References](#references) at the end.*

Two of Medicaid's new rules for expansion adults, the work requirement and renewals every six months instead of once a year, don't apply to American Indian and Alaska Native people. On paper, that sounds simple. In practice, it isn't: the federal definition of who counts is much broader than tribal enrollment, has nothing to do with race, and is easy for state eligibility systems to get wrong.

States are already taking very different approaches. KFF Health News reported that North Carolina accepts proof such as a tribal enrollment card, a tribal census record, or confirmation from the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians' Tribal Option, while Montana told reporters that people who declare their tribal status won't need more proof.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-1" id="cite-1" aria-label="Reference 1">1</a></sup> The stakes are high. The last time eligibility checks tightened, the National Council of Urban Indian Health estimated that more than 850,000 American Indians and Alaska Natives lost Medicaid by May 2024.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-2" id="cite-2" aria-label="Reference 2">2</a></sup>

Even a sensible state rule can break down at the state line. The Tribal Option is a care management program created by the Cherokee Indian Hospital Authority and offered mainly in five western North Carolina counties.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-3" id="cite-3" aria-label="Reference 3">3</a></sup> Its confirmation counts in North Carolina, but it won't help an Eastern Band citizen on Medicaid in another state unless that state accepts it. The same gap faces any tribal citizen whose state Medicaid agency has no working relationship with their tribe. As Liz Malerba, director of policy and legislative affairs for the United South and Eastern Tribes, put it: "A Navajo Nation citizen living in Connecticut, for example, may have a difficult time being identified by the state."<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">1</a></sup> About 87% of Native people live outside what the Census Bureau calls tribal statistical areas, such as reservations.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-4" id="cite-4" aria-label="Reference 4">4</a></sup> Because the federal definition covers members of every federally recognized tribe, wherever the tribe is based, a state that recognizes only in-state tribes or in-state records will miss people the rule exempts.

## If you are American Indian or Alaska Native

**Do these rules apply to me?** Not if you meet the federal definition of "Indian." You don't have to meet the work requirement, and your renewals stay once a year.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-5" id="cite-5" aria-label="Reference 5">5</a>,<a href="#ref-6" id="cite-6" aria-label="Reference 6">6</a>,<a href="#ref-7" id="cite-7" aria-label="Reference 7">7</a></sup> You don't need to live on a reservation or belong to a tribe based in your state. There are four ways to qualify, listed in the next section.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-8" aria-label="Reference 8">8</a></sup>

**Do I need to do anything right now?** Ideally, no. Your state is supposed to check the records it already has before it asks you for paperwork.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> Arizona says this plainly to its members: "If AHCCCS already has this information, you will be exempt automatically and do not need to take any extra steps."<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-22" id="cite-22" aria-label="Reference 22">22</a></sup>

**What should I watch for?** Make sure your Medicaid office has your current address, phone number and email, and open every letter it sends. That is how you'll hear if your state needs anything from you, and Arizona gives its members the same advice.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-22" aria-label="Reference 22">22</a></sup>

**What if the state asks for proof I don't have?** Don't panic. A state can't deny or end your coverage only because you lack documents.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> [What proof can I show?](#what-proof-can-i-show) lists what works. For help, contact your tribe's health program, an Indian Health Service or tribal clinic, or an urban Indian health program. You can find a facility on the [IHS health care map](https://www.ihs.gov/findhealthcare/)<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-23" id="cite-23" aria-label="Reference 23">23</a></sup> and an urban Indian organization through the [IHS Office of Urban Indian Health Programs](https://www.ihs.gov/urban/).<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-24" id="cite-24" aria-label="Reference 24">24</a></sup>

## Who qualifies

The federal definition has four routes. You qualify if **any one** of these routes is true:<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-8" id="cite-8" aria-label="Reference 8">8</a></sup>

| Route | Urban center required? | Recognized-tribe membership required? |
|---|---|---|
| Member of <strong>any</strong> federally recognized tribe, <strong>including tribes outside your state</strong>, and including an Alaska Native village or Alaska Native corporation<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-9" id="cite-9" aria-label="Reference 9">9</a></sup> | No | Yes |
| <strong>Or</strong> an urban Indian, which <strong>requires both</strong>:<ul class="route-logic"><li>Lives in an urban center</li><li><strong>And</strong> is any one of these:<ul><li>A member of <strong>any</strong> tribe, band or other organized group of Indians, <strong>including one based outside your state</strong>, a tribe terminated since 1940, or a tribe recognized by the state where the person lives</li><li><strong>Or</strong> a child or grandchild of such a member</li><li><strong>Or</strong> Eskimo, Aleut or other Alaska Native</li><li><strong>Or</strong> considered an Indian by Interior or under HHS rules<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-9" aria-label="Reference 9">9</a></sup></li></ul></li></ul> | Yes | No |
| <strong>Or</strong> considered an Indian by the Secretary of the Interior for any purpose, for example a person of one-half or more Indian blood<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-10" id="cite-10" aria-label="Reference 10">10</a></sup> | No | No |
| <strong>Or</strong> considered by HHS to be an Indian for purposes of Indian health care eligibility, including California Indians and Alaska Natives<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-11" id="cite-11" aria-label="Reference 11">11</a>,<a href="#ref-12" id="cite-12" aria-label="Reference 12">12</a></sup> | No | No; IHS looks at descent and community recognition |

A few terms:

- **Federally recognized tribe:** a tribe the United States recognizes as eligible for the programs and services it provides to Indians because of their status as Indians. The Interior Department publishes the list.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-9" aria-label="Reference 9">9</a></sup>
- **Terminated tribe:** a tribe whose federal recognition the United States ended. The urban route counts members of tribes terminated since 1940.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-9" aria-label="Reference 9">9</a></sup>
- **State-recognized tribe:** a tribe recognized by a state but not by the federal government. The urban route counts tribes recognized by the state where the person lives.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-9" aria-label="Reference 9">9</a></sup>
- **Urban center:** a community HHS has found to have enough urban Indians with unmet health needs to qualify for urban Indian health programs. It is not every city.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-9" aria-label="Reference 9">9</a></sup>

## What proof can I show?

Your state should check its own records first, such as your Medicaid case file and claims from Indian health providers.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" id="cite-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> If it still asks, these can show you qualify:<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-14" id="cite-14" aria-label="Reference 14">14</a></sup>

- **Tribal member:** a tribal enrollment card or membership letter, a tribal census record, or a document from your Alaska Native village or corporation.
- **Urban Indian:** proof of where you live, such as a lease, utility bill or voter card, and a tribal document showing your, your parent's or your grandparent's membership.
- **Considered an Indian by Interior:** a Certificate of Degree of Indian Blood (CDIB) from the Bureau of Indian Affairs, or a tribal enrollment document.
- **Eligible for Indian health care:** a record that you have received, or can receive, care from an Indian health provider. California Indians can also use a Bureau of Indian Affairs or tribal document for their basis.

Without documents, the state can't deny or end your coverage for that reason alone. It can accept other information instead, and from 1 January 2028 it must do so when no documents are reasonably available.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> Checking "American Indian or Alaska Native" on a form doesn't decide it either way.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-8" aria-label="Reference 8">8</a></sup> If your state's form or notice describes only tribal members, federal law still covers the other three routes.

## What states must do first

The state must check the records it already has before asking you for anything. It can't deny you only because documents don't exist, and once it finds you qualify, it can't check again before your next renewal unless something changed.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup>

Checking the race box on an application doesn't qualify anyone, because the exclusion turns on the legal statuses above, not race. States may require only the information needed to decide eligibility, and race isn't part of that,<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-15" id="cite-15" aria-label="Reference 15">15</a></sup> so the race question can't be relied on to find everyone who qualifies. Federal regulation sets three verification rules:<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup>

| Rule | What it requires |
|---|---|
| Data first | Check reliable information the state already holds, including its eligibility system, case records and claims from the past 12 months, before asking the person<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> |
| No documents that don't exist | When the state's own data fall short: before 1 January 2028 it may accept other information; from then on it must require documentation when reasonably available; when none is reasonably available, it must accept other information; it may never deny or end coverage only because documents don't exist<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> |
| No rechecking | Once excluded, a person is not re-verified before the next scheduled renewal (redetermination) unless the state learns something changed.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> CMS's preamble goes further: in its explanation published with the rule, CMS says states may not reverify American Indian status at all<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-16" id="cite-16" aria-label="Reference 16">16</a></sup> |

On 13 November 2026, CMS offered states an optional "hold harmless" period through 30 June 2027.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-17" id="cite-17" aria-label="Reference 17">17</a></sup> It's optional guidance, not a requirement. With CMS approval, a state can pause denials and disenrollments for people who haven't yet shown they qualify. No one is cut off during that time just because their exemption hasn't been confirmed. It buys time to fix verification but doesn't change who qualifies. States that want it from 1 January should tell CMS by 11 December 2026.

## Where eligibility systems go wrong

Most failures come from a few design choices, and the most common is treating the exemption as one yes-or-no flag. Each row below names a choice, what happens to people, and what the system should do instead. None of these fixes requires a state to collect tribal enrollment records, which belong to the Nations.

| Mistake | What happens | What the system should do |
|---|---|---|
| Race box as the test | Excludes some people the rule doesn't reach and misses many it does | Never use the race box as the test |
| One yes-or-no flag | Urban members, descendants, Alaska Natives and California Indians get missed upstream | Accept a finding that the person meets the definition from a source able to make it, and record the source |
| Ignoring state data | Indian health provider claims and the Indian status the state already records for the cost-sharing exemption go unused, though the rule counts the state's eligibility system and 12 months of claims as reliable information<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> | Check what the state holds first. An Indian health provider's claim is evidence CMS lists, but not proof on its own, since those providers may also serve some non-Indians, such as members' children and spouses<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-18" id="cite-18" aria-label="Reference 18">18</a></sup> |
| Asking again every check | The exclusion drops whenever the data feed is quiet | Keep the exclusion on record until the next redetermination |
| Outage read as "no" | A failed data source looks the same as a member who didn't comply | Record a failed source as "unknown" |
| No documents, no exclusion | Members without documents are sent to report hours | Accept other information under criteria the state sets |
| Six-month renewals for everyone | Members who qualify are renewed every six months, with more paperwork and more chances to lose coverage | Keep annual renewals for anyone who meets the definition |

## Questions to ask

A system's behavior depends on its configuration as much as its code. Ask these before go-live and after each change:

| Who asks | Question |
|---|---|
| State staff, to the vendor | Where is the federal definition applied, and by whom? |
| State staff, to the vendor | Can the system accept a finding from an Indian health provider or an IHS eligibility determination, and record its source? |
| State staff, to the vendor | Which agency records does it check before asking the member? |
| State staff, to the vendor | What happens to a member from an out-of-state tribe, an urban resident, or a descendant with no record on file? |
| State staff, to the vendor | Does the exclusion carry forward to the next check? |
| State staff, to the vendor | What does the system record when a data source fails? |
| State staff, to the vendor | Does the system keep annual renewals for people who meet the definition? |
| Indian health programs, to the state | Which of our records will you rely on beyond claims? |
| Indian health programs, to the state | Will you accept a finding without enrollment or descent records? |
| Indian health programs, to the state | How are patients told what to send, and is a patient without documents turned away? |
| Indian health programs, to the state | What happens to the exclusion if a data source is down? |
| Indian health programs, to the state | Will you use the CMS hold-harmless period, and what will you fix in verification before it ends? |

The vendor's answers belong in the state's verification plan.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a>,<a href="#ref-19" id="cite-19" aria-label="Reference 19">19</a></sup> Where Indian health programs or urban Indian organizations provide care, the state must also seek their advice before submitting any plan amendment or waiver likely to directly affect them.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-20" id="cite-20" aria-label="Reference 20">20</a></sup>

## What the rule leaves open

The rule tells states to use their own data, but it gives tribal health systems no defined role in verification.

Urban programs face the hardest case. Many of their patients belong to tribes in other states, descend from members, or belong to state-recognized or terminated tribes, and the state holds no tribal record for them. What leaves your program is your decision: we found nothing in the rule's verification requirements that requires sending a state your patients' tribal records.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup>

That leaves a gap, and it helps to keep three things apart.

**What federal law requires (binding):** the exemption itself, for everyone who meets the federal definition; and that states use the reliable information they already hold before asking a person for more. When those data fall short, a state may still ask the person. Nothing in the verification rule requires a state to set up data sharing with tribal health systems.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup>

**What CMS says beyond the regulation text:** in the rule's preamble, that states may not reverify a person's American Indian or Alaska Native status at all, which goes further than the regulation's own text (see the note below on this conflict);<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-16" aria-label="Reference 16">16</a></sup> and, in a 13 November 2026 letter to states, an optional hold-harmless period through 30 June 2027.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-17" aria-label="Reference 17">17</a></sup>

**What tribal advocates request (not binding):** the Tribal Technical Advisory Group asked CMS to "advise states to share data with Tribal entities within their borders."<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-21" id="cite-21" aria-label="Reference 21">21</a></sup> That is a request, not a rule.

So the regulation creates the exemption and a duty to check the state's own data. But its text sets up no way to connect state eligibility systems with the tribal health organizations that often know their patients best. Native Medicaid coverage shouldn't depend on whether one person can navigate a fragmented state verification process when reliable information may already exist within tribal health systems.

## How each state measures up

Our [state scorecard](/aian-medicaid-scorecard/) checks every state's published policy against 10 requirements drawn from these rules. It quotes and links the source for every finding, and it updates as states publish new policy. As of 20 November 2026, only 2 of the 44 states subject to these rules meet all 10 in their published policy. The median state meets 4, and 15 publish policy that contradicts at least one. Each state's page lists exactly what it would take to raise its grade, with model language from a state that already gets it right.

**A conflict in CMS's own rules.** On re-checking, CMS says three different things. The regulation bars checking a person's status again *between* regularly scheduled renewals, unless the state learns something changed.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> The preamble says states "may not reverify" American Indian status at all.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-16" aria-label="Reference 16">16</a></sup> CMS's training slides say status "cannot be verified more frequently than at each renewal."<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-25" id="cite-25" aria-label="Reference 25">25</a></sup> So a state that checks again at renewal has a plausible reading of the regulation, but it acts against CMS's stated interpretation. Until CMS resolves this, our scorecard grades by the regulation: a check *between* renewals is a contradiction, and a published check *at* renewal is marked **disputed**. Disputed counts neither as met nor as a contradiction. We think CMS should settle it in favor of its preamble, so that no one's status is checked again once it is established.

### The most common mistakes in published policy

Most states fall short in the same few ways. The counts below are from the scorecard as of 20 November 2026, across the 44 states subject to these rules. "Contradicts" means the state's own published words conflict with the federal rule. "Disputed" means the state publishes a practice that conflicts with CMS's preamble but not with the regulation's text. "Silent" means we found no published policy either way, or the wording was too unclear to count (if there's any question, we round down). Silence isn't a violation, but members and caseworkers can't rely on a rule that isn't written down. Each state's page quotes and links its source.

| Mistake | States | Why people lose coverage | The fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Six-month renewals for everyone | Contradicts: 12 (CT, DE, HI, IL, IN, KY, MD, ME, MO, OH, RI, VT). Silent: 13 | Each renewal is one more chance to lose coverage over paperwork. Federal law keeps 12-month renewals for American Indians and Alaska Natives in the expansion group<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-6" aria-label="Reference 6">6</a>,<a href="#ref-7" aria-label="Reference 7">7</a></sup> | Say plainly that American Indians and Alaska Natives in the expansion group keep 12-month renewals |
| Checking status again | Contradicts (between renewals): 0. Disputed (at renewal): 9 (MI, MT, NE, OH, PA, RI, VA, VT, WV). Silent: 30 | Every new check is a chance to drop someone whose status hasn't changed. The regulation bars checking again between scheduled renewals;<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> CMS's preamble says states may not reverify at all<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-16" aria-label="Reference 16">16</a></sup> | Once a person's status is established, keep it on file and don't check it again |
| Too narrow a definition | Contradicts: 5 (DE, IN, KY, NJ, PA). Silent: 30 | These states describe the exemption as tribal membership only. That leaves out urban Indians, children and grandchildren of members, members of terminated or state-recognized tribes, California Indians and others eligible for IHS care. All of them meet the federal definition<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-8" aria-label="Reference 8">8</a>,<a href="#ref-9" aria-label="Reference 9">9</a>,<a href="#ref-11" aria-label="Reference 11">11</a>,<a href="#ref-12" aria-label="Reference 12">12</a></sup> | Publish all four routes of the federal definition |
| Documents required | Contradicts: 2 (OH, UT). Silent: 28 | Many people who qualify have no card or certificate. The rule bars denying coverage only because documents don't exist<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> | Accept other information, such as the person's own statement, and never deny only for missing documents |
| Asking the person first | Contradicts: 0. Silent: 16 | The state may already hold the answer, such as Indian status on file or claims from an Indian health provider. Asking first puts the burden on the person, and a missed letter can end coverage<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> | Check the state's own records first and ask only for what is missing |
| Silent on tribes based in other states | Contradicts: 0. Silent: 34, the most common gap | A member of a tribe based elsewhere has no record with the state. If staff or systems only know in-state tribes, that person is missed. The definition covers members of every federally recognized tribe<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-8" aria-label="Reference 8">8</a>,<a href="#ref-9" aria-label="Reference 9">9</a></sup> | Say that members of tribes based in other states are exempt, with the same ways to show it |
| Not publishing the exemption | Silent on tribal members: 4 (AR, MA, NH, TN). Silent on Alaska Natives: 8 (AR, DE, IN, KY, MA, NH, PA, TN) | Members can't learn they are exempt, and caseworkers have nothing to apply | Publish the exemption in member materials, naming Alaska Natives |

States, tribes and Indian health programs can send new evidence from any state's page: choose "Send a published policy link", share a public link to the policy, and we review it within 10 business days. If your vendor, tribal liaison or health director needs this, send it on.

Stating the rule correctly is only the first step. A grade measures what a state has published, not what its eligibility system does. The exemption protects people only if the system applies it: it checks the state's own data first, doesn't re-check a person's status before the next renewal, keeps renewals at 12 months,<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-6" aria-label="Reference 6">6</a>,<a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> and records a failed data match as "unknown," not "no." That next step applies to every state, including the two that earn an A.
