*Last reviewed 19 November 2026. We will update this post when the rule or state policies change.*

Two of Medicaid's new rules for expansion adults don't apply to American Indian and Alaska Native people: the work requirement, and renewals every six months instead of every twelve. But the legal definition is broader than tribal enrollment, it has nothing to do with race, and eligibility systems can get it wrong.

States are already doing this differently. [KFF Health News reported](https://kffhealthnews.org/health-care-costs/medicaid-work-rule-requirement-native-american-north-carolina-exemption-coverage/) on 13 November 2026 that North Carolina requires a tribal enrollment card, a tribal census record, or confirmation from the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians' Tribal Option, while Montana says people who declare their tribal status won't need more proof. The last time eligibility checks tightened, the [National Council of Urban Indian Health estimated](https://ncuih.org/wp-content/uploads/Impact-Medicaid_NCUIH_D329_F4.pdf) that over 850,000 American Indians and Alaska Natives were terminated from Medicaid by May 2024.

Proof that works in one state may not travel. Confirmation from the Eastern Band's Tribal Option, a [care management program](https://medicaid.ncdhhs.gov/fact-sheet-eastern-band-cherokee-indians-tribal-option-overview/open) created by the Cherokee Indian Hospital Authority and offered mainly in five western North Carolina counties, counts in North Carolina. It won't help an Eastern Band citizen on Medicaid in another state unless that state accepts it, or a citizen of any tribe whose state Medicaid agency has no working relationship with that tribe. Liz Malerba, director of policy and legislative affairs for the United South and Eastern Tribes, told KFF Health News: "A Navajo Nation citizen living in Connecticut, for example, may have a difficult time being identified by the state." KFF Health News, citing 2020 data published by the HHS Office of Minority Health, reports that about 87% of Native people live outside what the Census Bureau calls tribal statistical areas, such as reservations. The federal definition covers members of every federally recognized tribe, wherever the tribe is based, so a state that recognizes only in-state tribes or in-state records will miss people the rule exempts.

This post is for state eligibility staff, their vendors, and leaders of Indian health and urban Indian programs. In short:

- Four routes qualify for both exemptions, and only one needs membership in a federally recognized tribe.
- Most failures come from treating the exemption as a single yes-or-no flag.
- Only 3 of the 44 states subject to these rules meet all 10 requirements in our [state scorecard](/aian-medicaid-scorecard/).

## The rule

For the work requirement, a person is excluded if they "meet the definition of Indian at § 447.51" ([42 CFR 435.554(c)(2)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/03/2026-11094/medicaid-program-community-engagement-requirement-for-certain-individuals)). The six-month renewal exemption covers the same groups in the statute, so they keep annual renewals (Social Security Act section 1902(e)(14)(L)(ii); CMS letter [SMD 26-001](https://www.medicaid.gov/federal-policy-guidance/downloads/smd26001.pdf)). The definition has four routes, and only the first requires membership in a federally recognized tribe:

| Route ([42 CFR 447.51](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/447.51)) | Urban center required? | Recognized-tribe membership required? |
|---|---|---|
| Member of a federally recognized tribe, including an Alaska Native village or Alaska Native corporation | No | Yes |
| Urban Indian: lives in an urban center (a community HHS designates; [25 U.S.C. 1603(27)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/25/1603)) and is a member of any tribe, band or group (including terminated or state-recognized ones) or a first- or second-degree descendant of a member; or is Eskimo, Aleut or other Alaska Native; or is considered an Indian by Interior or under HHS rules | Yes | No |
| Considered an Indian by the Secretary of the Interior for any purpose, for example a person of one-half or more Indian blood ([25 U.S.C. 5129](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/25/5129)) | No | No |
| Considered by HHS to be an Indian for purposes of Indian health care eligibility (see [42 CFR 136.12](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/136.12)), including California Indians ([25 U.S.C. 1679](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/25/1679)) and Alaska Natives | No | No; IHS looks at descent and community recognition |

The race box on an application doesn't qualify anyone: the exclusion turns on the legal statuses above, not race. States may require only the information needed to decide eligibility ([42 CFR 435.907(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/435.907)), and race isn't part of that, so the race question can't be relied on to find everyone who qualifies. How the state verifies matters as much as the definition ([42 CFR 435.557](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/435.557), as [corrected on 29 June 2026](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-06-29/pdf/C1-2026-11094.pdf)):

| Rule | What it requires | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Data first | Check reliable information the state already holds, including its eligibility system, case records and claims from the past 12 months, before asking the person | 435.557(a), (b), (c)(1) |
| No documents that don't exist | When the state's own data fall short: before 1 January 2028 it may accept other information; from then on it must require documentation when reasonably available; when none is reasonably available, it must accept other information; it may never deny or end coverage only because documents don't exist | 435.557(b)(2) |
| No rechecking | Once excluded, a person is not re-verified before the next scheduled redetermination unless the state learns something changed. The rule's preamble goes further: for American Indians, CMS says states may not reverify the status at all (91 FR 33366) | 435.557(d)(4) |

On 13 November 2026, CMS offered states an optional "hold harmless" period through 30 June 2027 ([SMD 26-006](https://www.medicaid.gov/Federal-Policy-Guidance/Downloads/smd26006.pdf)). With CMS approval, a state can pause denials and disenrollments for people who haven't yet shown they qualify. It buys time to fix verification but changes nothing about who qualifies. States that want it from 1 January should tell CMS by 11 December 2026.

## Where systems go wrong

None of these fixes requires a state to collect tribal enrollment records, which belong to the Nations.

| Mistake | What happens | What the system should do |
|---|---|---|
| Race box as the test | Excludes some people the rule doesn't reach and misses many it does | Never use the race box as the test |
| One yes-or-no flag | Urban members, descendants, Alaska Natives and California Indians get missed upstream | Accept a finding that the person meets 447.51 from a source able to make it, and record the source |
| Ignoring state data | Indian health provider claims and the Indian status the state already records for the cost-sharing exemption go unused, though the rule counts the state's eligibility system and 12 months of claims as reliable information (435.557(a)(iv), (vii)) | Check what the state holds first. An Indian health provider's claim is evidence CMS lists, but not proof on its own, since those providers may also serve some non-Indians, such as members' children and spouses ([25 U.S.C. 1680c](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/25/1680c)) |
| Asking again every check | The exclusion drops whenever the data feed is quiet | Keep the exclusion on record until the next redetermination |
| Outage read as "no" | A failed data source looks the same as a member who didn't comply | Record a failed source as "unknown" |
| No documents, no exclusion | Members without documents are sent to report hours | Accept other information under criteria the state sets |
| Six-month renewals for everyone | Members who qualify are renewed every six months, with more paperwork and more chances to lose coverage | Keep annual renewals for anyone who meets the definition |

## Questions to ask

A system's behavior depends on its configuration as much as its code. Ask these before go-live and after each change:

| Who asks | Question |
|---|---|
| State staff, to the vendor | Where is the 447.51 definition applied, and by whom? |
| State staff, to the vendor | Can the system accept a finding from an Indian health provider or an IHS eligibility determination, and record its source? |
| State staff, to the vendor | Which agency records does it check before asking the member? |
| State staff, to the vendor | What happens to a member from an out-of-state tribe, an urban resident, or a descendant with no record on file? |
| State staff, to the vendor | Does the exclusion carry forward to the next check? |
| State staff, to the vendor | What does the system record when a data source fails? |
| State staff, to the vendor | Does the system keep annual renewals for people who meet the definition? |
| Indian health programs, to the state | Which of our records will you rely on beyond claims? |
| Indian health programs, to the state | Will you accept a finding without enrollment or descent records? |
| Indian health programs, to the state | How are patients told what to send, and is a patient without documents turned away? |
| Indian health programs, to the state | What happens to the exclusion if a data source is down? |
| Indian health programs, to the state | Will you use the CMS hold-harmless period, and what will you fix in verification before it ends? |

The vendor's answers belong in the state's verification plan (435.557(b)(1)(iii) and [435.945(j)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/435.945)), and in states where Indian health programs or urban Indian organizations provide care, the state must seek their advice before submitting any plan amendment or waiver likely to directly affect them ([Social Security Act section 1902(a)(73)](https://www.ssa.gov/OP_Home/ssact/title19/1902.htm)).

Urban programs face the hardest case: many patients belong to tribes in other states, descend from members, or belong to state-recognized or terminated tribes, and the state holds no tribal record for them. What leaves your program is your decision; we found nothing in the rule's verification requirements (435.557) that requires sending a state your patients' tribal records.

That leaves a gap, and it helps to keep three things apart.

**What federal law requires:** the exemption itself, for everyone who meets the 447.51 definition; and that states use the reliable information they already hold before asking a person for more (435.557). When those data fall short, a state may still ask the person. Nothing in 435.557 requires a state to set up data sharing with tribal health systems.

**What CMS advises:** in the rule's preamble (91 FR 33366), that states may not reverify a person's American Indian or Alaska Native status at all, which goes further than the regulation's own text; and, in a 13 November 2026 letter to states ([SMD 26-006](https://www.medicaid.gov/Federal-Policy-Guidance/Downloads/smd26006.pdf)), an optional hold-harmless period through 30 June 2027.

**What tribal advocates request:** the Tribal Technical Advisory Group asked CMS to "advise states to share data with Tribal entities within their borders" ([letter of 8 July 2026](https://www.nihb.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/TTAG-Letter-Re-CMS-2454-IFC-Final.pdf)). That is a request, not a rule.

So the regulation creates the exemption and a duty to check the state's own data, but its text sets up no mechanism connecting state eligibility systems with the tribal health organizations that often know their patients best. Native Medicaid coverage shouldn't depend on whether one person can navigate a fragmented state verification process when reliable information may already exist within tribal health systems.

## How each state measures up

Our [state scorecard](/aian-medicaid-scorecard/) checks every state's published policy against 10 requirements drawn from these rules, quotes and links the source for every finding, and updates as states publish new policy. As of 18 November 2026, only 3 of the 44 states subject to these rules meet all 10 in their published policy. The median state meets 4, and 25 publish policy that contradicts at least one. Each state's page lists exactly what it would take to raise its grade, with model language from a state that already gets it right.

States, tribes and Indian health programs can send new evidence from any state's page. If your vendor, tribal liaison or health director needs this, send it on.
