*Last reviewed 19 November 2026. We will update this post when the rule or state policies change. Sources are numbered and listed under [References](#references) at the end.*

Two of Medicaid's new rules for expansion adults don't apply to American Indian and Alaska Native people: the work requirement, and renewals every six months instead of every twelve. But the legal definition is broader than tribal enrollment, it has nothing to do with race, and eligibility systems can get it wrong.

States are already doing this differently. North Carolina requires a tribal enrollment card, a tribal census record, or confirmation from the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians' Tribal Option, while Montana says people who declare their tribal status won't need more proof.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-1" id="cite-1" aria-label="Reference 1">1</a></sup> The last time eligibility checks tightened, the National Council of Urban Indian Health estimated that over 850,000 American Indians and Alaska Natives were terminated from Medicaid by May 2024.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-2" id="cite-2" aria-label="Reference 2">2</a></sup>

Proof that works in one state may not travel. Confirmation from the Eastern Band's Tribal Option, a care management program created by the Cherokee Indian Hospital Authority and offered mainly in five western North Carolina counties, counts in North Carolina.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-3" id="cite-3" aria-label="Reference 3">3</a></sup> It won't help an Eastern Band citizen on Medicaid in another state unless that state accepts it, or a citizen of any tribe whose state Medicaid agency has no working relationship with that tribe. As Liz Malerba, director of policy and legislative affairs for the United South and Eastern Tribes, put it: "A Navajo Nation citizen living in Connecticut, for example, may have a difficult time being identified by the state."<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-1" aria-label="Reference 1">1</a></sup> About 87% of Native people live outside what the Census Bureau calls tribal statistical areas, such as reservations.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-4" id="cite-4" aria-label="Reference 4">4</a></sup> The federal definition covers members of every federally recognized tribe, wherever the tribe is based, so a state that recognizes only in-state tribes or in-state records will miss people the rule exempts.

This post is for Native people checking whether they qualify, leaders of Indian health and urban Indian programs, and state eligibility staff and their vendors. In short:

- Four routes qualify for both exemptions, and only one needs membership in a federally recognized tribe.
- Most failures come from treating the exemption as a single yes-or-no flag.
- Only 3 of the 44 states subject to these rules meet all 10 requirements in our [state scorecard](/aian-medicaid-scorecard/).

Where to start:

- **If you want to know whether you qualify,** read [Who qualifies](#who-qualifies) and [Am I exempt, and what can I show?](#am-i-exempt-and-what-can-i-show)
- **If you lead an Indian health or urban Indian program,** read [Questions to ask](#questions-to-ask) and [What the rule leaves open](#what-the-rule-leaves-open).
- **If you run or build an eligibility system,** read [What states must do first](#what-states-must-do-first) and [Where eligibility systems go wrong](#where-eligibility-systems-go-wrong).

## Who qualifies

If you meet the federal definition of "Indian", you don't have to meet the work requirement,<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-5" id="cite-5" aria-label="Reference 5">5</a></sup> and your Medicaid renewals stay once a year instead of every six months.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-6" id="cite-6" aria-label="Reference 6">6</a>,<a href="#ref-7" id="cite-7" aria-label="Reference 7">7</a></sup> The definition has four routes, and a yes to any one is enough:<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-8" id="cite-8" aria-label="Reference 8">8</a></sup>

| Route | Urban center required? | Recognized-tribe membership required? |
|---|---|---|
| Member of a federally recognized tribe, including an Alaska Native village or Alaska Native corporation<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-9" id="cite-9" aria-label="Reference 9">9</a></sup> | No | Yes |
| Urban Indian: lives in an urban center and is a member of any tribe, band or group (including tribes terminated since 1940 or recognized by the state where the person lives) or a child or grandchild of a member; or is Eskimo, Aleut or other Alaska Native; or is considered an Indian by Interior or under HHS rules<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-9" aria-label="Reference 9">9</a></sup> | Yes | No |
| Considered an Indian by the Secretary of the Interior for any purpose, for example a person of one-half or more Indian blood<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-10" id="cite-10" aria-label="Reference 10">10</a></sup> | No | No |
| Considered by HHS to be an Indian for purposes of Indian health care eligibility, including California Indians and Alaska Natives<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-11" id="cite-11" aria-label="Reference 11">11</a>,<a href="#ref-12" id="cite-12" aria-label="Reference 12">12</a></sup> | No | No; IHS looks at descent and community recognition |

A few terms:

- **Federally recognized tribe:** a tribe the United States recognizes as eligible for the programs and services it provides to Indians because of their status as Indians. The Interior Department publishes the list.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-9" aria-label="Reference 9">9</a></sup>
- **Terminated tribe:** a tribe whose federal recognition the United States ended. The urban route counts members of tribes terminated since 1940.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-9" aria-label="Reference 9">9</a></sup>
- **State-recognized tribe:** a tribe recognized by a state but not by the federal government. The urban route counts tribes recognized by the state where the person lives.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-9" aria-label="Reference 9">9</a></sup>
- **Urban center:** a community HHS has found to have enough urban Indians with unmet health needs to qualify for urban Indian health programs. It is not every city.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-9" aria-label="Reference 9">9</a></sup>

## Am I exempt, and what can I show?

Your state should check its own records first, such as your Medicaid case file and claims from Indian health providers, before asking you for anything.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" id="cite-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> If it asks, these can show you qualify:<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-14" id="cite-14" aria-label="Reference 14">14</a></sup>

- **Tribal member:** a tribal enrollment card or membership letter, a tribal census record, or a document from your Alaska Native village or corporation.
- **Urban Indian:** proof of where you live, such as a lease, utility bill or voter card, and a tribal document showing your, your parent's or your grandparent's membership.
- **Considered an Indian by Interior:** a Certificate of Degree of Indian Blood (CDIB) from the Bureau of Indian Affairs, or a tribal enrollment document.
- **Eligible for Indian health care:** a record that you have received, or can receive, care from an Indian health provider. California Indians can also use a Bureau of Indian Affairs or tribal document for their basis.

If you don't have documents, the state can't deny or end your coverage only for that. It can accept other information instead, and from 1 January 2028 it must do so when no documents are reasonably available.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> Checking "American Indian or Alaska Native" on a form doesn't decide it either way.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-8" aria-label="Reference 8">8</a></sup> If your state's form or notice describes only tribal members, federal law still covers the other three routes.

## What states must do first

The race box on an application doesn't qualify anyone: the exclusion turns on the legal statuses above, not race. States may require only the information needed to decide eligibility, and race isn't part of that,<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-15" id="cite-15" aria-label="Reference 15">15</a></sup> so the race question can't be relied on to find everyone who qualifies. How the state verifies matters as much as the definition:<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup>

| Rule | What it requires |
|---|---|
| Data first | Check reliable information the state already holds, including its eligibility system, case records and claims from the past 12 months, before asking the person<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> |
| No documents that don't exist | When the state's own data fall short: before 1 January 2028 it may accept other information; from then on it must require documentation when reasonably available; when none is reasonably available, it must accept other information; it may never deny or end coverage only because documents don't exist<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> |
| No rechecking | Once excluded, a person is not re-verified before the next scheduled redetermination unless the state learns something changed.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> The rule's preamble goes further: for American Indians, CMS says states may not reverify the status at all<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-16" id="cite-16" aria-label="Reference 16">16</a></sup> |

On 13 November 2026, CMS offered states an optional "hold harmless" period through 30 June 2027.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-17" id="cite-17" aria-label="Reference 17">17</a></sup> With CMS approval, a state can pause denials and disenrollments for people who haven't yet shown they qualify. It buys time to fix verification but changes nothing about who qualifies. States that want it from 1 January should tell CMS by 11 December 2026.

## Where eligibility systems go wrong

None of these fixes requires a state to collect tribal enrollment records, which belong to the Nations.

| Mistake | What happens | What the system should do |
|---|---|---|
| Race box as the test | Excludes some people the rule doesn't reach and misses many it does | Never use the race box as the test |
| One yes-or-no flag | Urban members, descendants, Alaska Natives and California Indians get missed upstream | Accept a finding that the person meets the definition from a source able to make it, and record the source |
| Ignoring state data | Indian health provider claims and the Indian status the state already records for the cost-sharing exemption go unused, though the rule counts the state's eligibility system and 12 months of claims as reliable information<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> | Check what the state holds first. An Indian health provider's claim is evidence CMS lists, but not proof on its own, since those providers may also serve some non-Indians, such as members' children and spouses<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-18" id="cite-18" aria-label="Reference 18">18</a></sup> |
| Asking again every check | The exclusion drops whenever the data feed is quiet | Keep the exclusion on record until the next redetermination |
| Outage read as "no" | A failed data source looks the same as a member who didn't comply | Record a failed source as "unknown" |
| No documents, no exclusion | Members without documents are sent to report hours | Accept other information under criteria the state sets |
| Six-month renewals for everyone | Members who qualify are renewed every six months, with more paperwork and more chances to lose coverage | Keep annual renewals for anyone who meets the definition |

## Questions to ask

A system's behavior depends on its configuration as much as its code. Ask these before go-live and after each change:

| Who asks | Question |
|---|---|
| State staff, to the vendor | Where is the federal definition applied, and by whom? |
| State staff, to the vendor | Can the system accept a finding from an Indian health provider or an IHS eligibility determination, and record its source? |
| State staff, to the vendor | Which agency records does it check before asking the member? |
| State staff, to the vendor | What happens to a member from an out-of-state tribe, an urban resident, or a descendant with no record on file? |
| State staff, to the vendor | Does the exclusion carry forward to the next check? |
| State staff, to the vendor | What does the system record when a data source fails? |
| State staff, to the vendor | Does the system keep annual renewals for people who meet the definition? |
| Indian health programs, to the state | Which of our records will you rely on beyond claims? |
| Indian health programs, to the state | Will you accept a finding without enrollment or descent records? |
| Indian health programs, to the state | How are patients told what to send, and is a patient without documents turned away? |
| Indian health programs, to the state | What happens to the exclusion if a data source is down? |
| Indian health programs, to the state | Will you use the CMS hold-harmless period, and what will you fix in verification before it ends? |

The vendor's answers belong in the state's verification plan.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a>,<a href="#ref-19" id="cite-19" aria-label="Reference 19">19</a></sup> In states where Indian health programs or urban Indian organizations provide care, the state must also seek their advice before submitting any plan amendment or waiver likely to directly affect them.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-20" id="cite-20" aria-label="Reference 20">20</a></sup>

## What the rule leaves open

Urban programs face the hardest case: many patients belong to tribes in other states, descend from members, or belong to state-recognized or terminated tribes, and the state holds no tribal record for them. What leaves your program is your decision. We found nothing in the rule's verification requirements that requires sending a state your patients' tribal records.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup>

That leaves a gap, and it helps to keep three things apart.

**What federal law requires:** the exemption itself, for everyone who meets the federal definition; and that states use the reliable information they already hold before asking a person for more. When those data fall short, a state may still ask the person. Nothing in the verification rule requires a state to set up data sharing with tribal health systems.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-13" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup>

**What CMS advises:** in the rule's preamble, that states may not reverify a person's American Indian or Alaska Native status at all, which goes further than the regulation's own text;<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-16" aria-label="Reference 16">16</a></sup> and, in a 13 November 2026 letter to states, an optional hold-harmless period through 30 June 2027.<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-17" aria-label="Reference 17">17</a></sup>

**What tribal advocates request:** the Tribal Technical Advisory Group asked CMS to "advise states to share data with Tribal entities within their borders."<sup class="fn-ref"><a href="#ref-21" id="cite-21" aria-label="Reference 21">21</a></sup> That is a request, not a rule.

So the regulation creates the exemption and a duty to check the state's own data, but its text sets up no mechanism connecting state eligibility systems with the tribal health organizations that often know their patients best. Native Medicaid coverage shouldn't depend on whether one person can navigate a fragmented state verification process when reliable information may already exist within tribal health systems.

## How each state measures up

Our [state scorecard](/aian-medicaid-scorecard/) checks every state's published policy against 10 requirements drawn from these rules, quotes and links the source for every finding, and updates as states publish new policy. As of 18 November 2026, only 3 of the 44 states subject to these rules meet all 10 in their published policy. The median state meets 4, and 25 publish policy that contradicts at least one. Each state's page lists exactly what it would take to raise its grade, with model language from a state that already gets it right.

States, tribes and Indian health programs can send new evidence from any state's page: choose "Submit evidence", share a public link to the policy, and we review it within 10 business days. If your vendor, tribal liaison or health director needs this, send it on.
