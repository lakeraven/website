# How States Must Verify the Exemption

<p class="usa-intro">Data first, enroll promptly, no paperwork traps, no rechecking.</p>

*Last reviewed October 2026. Part of our guide to the [American Indian and Alaska Native exemption](/medicaid-native-exemption/). Not legal advice.*

---

Qualifying for the exemption is one step. The state also has to recognize it. The federal verification rule, [42 CFR 435.557](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/section-435.557), sets out how states must do that.

---

## 1. Check the data the state already has

States must identify their data sources, list them in a verification plan, and use them **before** asking the person for more ([435.557(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/section-435.557)). "Reliable information available to the State" includes electronic data sources, information from other agencies, and claims and encounter data from the past 12 months ([435.557(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/section-435.557)).

At application and renewal, the state must check all reliable information before asking for more, and it may not limit which information it checks ([435.557(c)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/section-435.557)). When it has enough information, it must find the person excluded ([435.557(c)(2)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/section-435.557)).

**Why it matters:** many states already hold information that points to Indian status, such as an earlier AI/AN determination, Medicaid claims from IHS or tribal facilities, or a prior exemption from cost sharing.

## 2. Enroll first, then confirm

If the information supports eligibility but more is needed to confirm an exclusion, the state must enroll the person promptly and verify the exclusion afterward ([435.557(c)(3)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/section-435.557)).

## 3. Don't turn the exemption into a paperwork test

Before January 1, 2028, a state may accept other information instead of requiring documents ([435.557(b)(2)(i)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/section-435.557)). From January 1, 2028, it must require documentation when documentation is reasonably available ([435.557(b)(2)(ii)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/section-435.557)).

Under either rule, a state may not deny or end coverage only because a person can't produce a document that doesn't exist or isn't reasonably available ([435.557(b)(2)(iii)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/section-435.557)). Many people who qualify, such as descendants and California Indians, have no single card that proves it.

## 4. Don't recheck a confirmed exemption

Once a person is found excluded, the state may not reverify that status between regularly scheduled renewals unless it has information that the status changed ([435.557(d)(4)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/section-435.557)). Indian status rarely changes. Guidance that says exemptions must be "verified every 6 months" deserves a close look.

---

## Where tribes and Indian health programs fit

Tribes decide who their members are, and IHS and tribal programs already decide who is eligible for Indian health services. Those are the most reliable sources for this exemption. The federal rule lets states use information from other agencies and from claims and encounter data. A state can choose to accept confirmation from a tribe or an Indian health program, and tribes can ask for that in consultation.

Any such arrangement should respect tribal data sovereignty. A tribe or clinic should decide what it shares, and a state should need no more than a yes or no answer.

---

## Questions to ask your state

1. Which data sources will you check for Indian status before contacting the person?
2. Does your guidance use the full 447.51 definition, including urban Indians, descendants, California Indians and IHS eligibility?
3. What can a person provide if they have no enrollment card?
4. Will you accept confirmation from a tribe, IHS, a tribal health program or an urban Indian organization?
5. Will you recheck excluded status between renewals? If so, under what authority?
